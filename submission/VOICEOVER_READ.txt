What if the audience wrote the monster—and the one weakness that could bring it down?

This is ARAGOZ: an Egyptian puppet-theatre-inspired boss fight. Chat makes the monster. You find the loophole.

In this real Twitch test, a chat command proposed a charge that cannot turn. A vote selected it, and the author’s name stayed attached to the rule. Empty choices are clearly labelled House Suggestions.

Here is how that rule plays. Once the boss commits, it cannot steer. Put a striped stage block in its path. The collision jams the axle and opens a counter. Strike the exposed attachment, and the wheel breaks away. Charging stops because the module—and its damage—are disabled.

The real Twitch result carries the same credit: selection, activation, break, and showbill. This demonstration used one broadcaster account operated by our agent. It is a technical test, not a crowd playtest.

A different module changes the puzzle. Steam overheats after its burst sequence, exposing a valve. Delayed echo records your movement, previews the path, then copies it. Lead that copy into a block to expose the mirror.

The show runs for three acts. Encore keeps the exact recipe. Remix replaces one module. A quiet channel can play with House Suggestions, and anyone can rehearse without a Twitch account.

I built ARAGOZ with Codex across the game, server, original Blender assets, and validation. The simulation runs at sixty fixed steps a second, separate from menus and networking. Versioned contracts connect chat, recipes, modules, events, and results. Codex also helped test immediate damage cancellation and recovery.

Live proposals currently match six authored profiles. Runtime model calls are disabled. Readable warnings, remapping, reduced motion, and mute controls keep the performance understandable and under the host’s control.

The goal is simple: let chat write a boast, let the player break it, and let everyone see whose idea just backfired.
